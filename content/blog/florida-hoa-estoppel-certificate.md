If you are buying or selling a home in a Florida community association, an **estoppel certificate** is the association's official, signed statement of exactly what the current owner owes — regular assessments, any special assessments, late fees, fines, and other charges — as of a specific date. Closing agents order it so that the buyer takes clear title and no unpaid balance follows the property. It is a public-record-adjacent document produced by the association under state law, and Florida statute controls what it must say, what it can cost, and how quickly it must be delivered.

## What does an estoppel certificate actually contain?

Florida law lists the specific items an estoppel certificate must disclose. In broad terms, it states the amount of the regular periodic assessment and how it is paid (monthly, quarterly, annually), the date through which assessments are paid, and the total of any other money the association claims is due as of the certificate date — special assessments, unpaid fines, interest, and collection or attorney charges tied to the parcel.

It also commonly includes information a buyer needs to step into the owner's shoes: whether there is a capital contribution, transfer, or similar fee due on the sale; whether the association has an approval right over the sale or lease; and the name and contact point for the association or its managing agent. Because the figures are certified, the document is not an estimate — it is a representation the association is legally bound to stand behind.

The exact required contents are set by **Section 720.30851 of the Florida Statutes** for homeowners' associations and **Section 718.116(8)** for condominium associations. Those statutes, not any third-party summary, are the controlling source for the current list of required disclosures.

## When is an estoppel certificate needed?

The certificate is almost always tied to a transaction: a sale, a refinance, or sometimes a transfer of title into a trust or between family members. A written request triggers the association's duty to produce it. In practice the request comes from the closing agent, title company, or the owner's or buyer's attorney rather than from the parties directly, because the certificate is slotted into the closing file alongside the title commitment and the payoff statements.

Timing matters on both ends. The request has to be made early enough for the statutory delivery window to run before the closing date, and the closing has to occur before the certificate expires. When a deal is rescheduled, the certificate often has to be refreshed.

## How much can a Florida association charge for it?

Florida statute **caps the preparation fee** an association or its management company can charge for a standard estoppel certificate, with a higher ceiling allowed when the owner's account is delinquent and an additional expedited fee allowed when the certificate is requested on a rush basis (delivered within three business days). The statutory caps are periodically adjusted, so the exact dollar amounts in effect for your closing should be confirmed against the current version of the statute or the association's management company rather than assumed from an older figure you may find online.

Two consumer-protective rules sit alongside the cap. First, if the association does not deliver the certificate within the statutory window after a proper request, it may not charge the preparation fee at all. Second, if the sale or refinance does not close, the owner can request a refund of the estoppel fee within a set period. Both rules come directly from the estoppel statutes.

## How fast must the association respond?

The statute requires the association to prepare and deliver the estoppel certificate within **10 business days** after receiving a written request. The certificate then carries a built-in validity period — 30 days if it was delivered electronically, or 35 days if delivered by mail — measured from its preparation date. If nothing in the certificate changes, the association generally cannot charge a new preparation fee to amend it during that window.

These deadlines are why closing agents order estoppels well ahead of the closing date. A certificate that arrives late, or that has expired by the time the deal closes, can hold up a transaction that is otherwise ready.

## What makes the document legally binding?

The word *estoppel* is a legal term meaning a party is barred from contradicting something it previously represented. That is exactly how the certificate works: once the association certifies the amount owed as of a given date, it is generally stopped from later coming after the new owner for a different, higher balance for that same period. The buyer and the closing agent are entitled to rely on the figure.

That reliance is the document's whole purpose, and it is why the information has to be accurate and why the statute makes the association responsible for producing it on a deadline. It converts a potentially open-ended question — *does this property carry hidden association debt?* — into a fixed, certified number the parties can close on.

## How do I verify the association and its manager?

An estoppel certificate is only as trustworthy as the entity issuing it, and in Florida that entity is a registered association, often working through a licensed management firm and an individual **Community Association Manager (CAM)**. Before you send a payment or rely on a certificate, it is reasonable to confirm that the association is on file with the state and that the firm or manager handling it holds a current license.

You can start that check in this registry. Search for the community by name to find its registration record, or look up the management firm or manager directly to confirm the entity exists and see its license status as recorded by the state.
